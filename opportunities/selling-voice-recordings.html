Selling voice recordings and voice-over work

$80–$600 high effort online No face needed

Narration, commercials, e-learning and audiobooks. Your face never appears, and the barrier is audio quality far more than vocal talent.

How the money works

Clients post projects and you audition. On marketplace platforms you are paid per completed project, with the platform taking commission and often charging for the ability to audition at all.

Time to first payment

Weeks to months. Auditioning is competitive and early rejection is the norm.

The room matters more than the voice

The most common reason a promising voice does not get hired is not tone or delivery. It is room echo. Clients hear reverb and reject the audio, often without articulating why.

This is good news, because a room is cheaper to fix than a voice is to acquire. Recording in a wardrobe surrounded by hanging clothes genuinely outperforms an expensive microphone in an empty room with hard walls. Spend on treatment before spending on gear.

Be careful with pay-to-audition economics

Voices operates a paid membership model. Secondary sources report $499/year for Premium and a 20% commission on completed projects, with a free tier that reportedly cannot proactively audition for most work.

We could not confirm either figure from Voices’ own pricing page, so treat both as unverified and check directly before paying.

The structural point holds regardless of the exact numbers: you are paying an annual fee for the right to compete, in a pool that includes full-time professionals with years of reviews. That can be a reasonable business expense, but it is an expense with an expected return, and it should be evaluated as one. Establish that you can win work before paying for better access to it.

The clause that now matters most

Whether a client may use your recordings to train a synthetic voice is a live commercial term. A licence granting perpetual synthesis rights can be worth vastly more than the fee attached to it, because it replaces every future job that voice would have done.

Read those clauses specifically. Do not assume a standard contract excludes it.

How to start

  1. Fix the room before the microphone. Clients reject audio for room echo far more often than for vocal quality. Soft furnishings, a wardrobe, or a duvet over a frame beat an expensive mic in a bare room.
  2. Record a short, genre-specific demo. One tight commercial demo outperforms a long reel that spans every genre.
  3. Test free and low-cost routes first. Prove you can win work before paying for a premium marketplace membership.
  4. Read AI-synthesis clauses before signing. Whether a client may train a synthetic voice on your recordings is now a central commercial term, not fine print.
  5. Audition consistently. This is a volume game early on. Sporadic auditioning produces no work at all.

Scams and risks to know

Pay-to-audition platforms

How it works: A site charges a substantial annual membership, then places you in a pool where established talent wins nearly everything.

How to avoid it: Treat membership as a business expense with an expected return, not a ticket. Test free routes first and verify current pricing on the platform itself.

Unpaid 'test' scripts

How it works: A client requests a long custom sample, then uses it without payment.

How to avoid it: Auditions are short. Any 'test' longer than about thirty seconds, or that reads like finished deliverable copy, is the job itself.

Voice cloning consent buried in terms

How it works: A contract grants perpetual rights to synthesise your voice.

How to avoid it: Read AI and synthesis clauses specifically. Your voice is a biometric asset and this clause is now common.

Good fit if

  • People with a quiet space and patience
  • Anyone who wants creative work with no face exposure
  • Those willing to invest modestly in equipment before earning

Not for you if

  • Anyone without a quiet recording environment
  • People needing income within weeks
  • Those unwilling to be rejected repeatedly

Eligibility

  • A clear speaking voice and reliable diction
  • A quiet recording space — this matters more than your voice
  • A microphone better than a laptop's built-in one
  • Willingness to be rejected repeatedly while building a reputation

Geography: International.
